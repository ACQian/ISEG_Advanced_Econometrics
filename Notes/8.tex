\setchapter{8}{The Bootstrap}
\ChapterHeading

The bootstrap is a method introduced by Efron (1979) to estimate the distribution of an estimator, its variance, or the distribution of a test statistic by resampling the data.
Suppose we have a sample $(X_1,\ldots,X_n)$ and we would like to construct a confidence interval for a parameter $\theta$.
We estiamte $\theta$ using a mathematical formula that depends on the sample $\hat{\theta} = T(X_1,\ldots,X_n)$, $T(\cdot)$ is a known function.
Let us assume that $\hat{\theta}$ is root-n consistent and $\hat{\theta}$ is asymptotically normal. That is $\sqrt{n}(\hat{\theta}-\theta)\xrightarrow{d}N(0,\lambda^2)$.
The \Emph{statistics of interest} are 
\begin{itemize}
    \item the estimator $\hat{\theta}$
    \item the standard errors of $\hat{\theta}$: $s_{\hat{\theta}}$ a consistent estimator of $\frac{\lambda}{\sqrt{n}}$
    \item the t-statistic $\hat{t} = \frac{(\hat{\theta}-\theta_{0})}{s_{\hat{\theta}}}$ where $H_0 : \theta = \theta_0$
    \item the associated critical value or p-value for the test
    \item confidence interval
\end{itemize}

\section{Basics on bootstrap}

Let $(X_1^*,\ldots,X_n^*)$ be a bootstrap sample generated from the original sample and $\hat{\theta}^* = T(X_1^*,\ldots,X_n^*)$.
Generate B different bootstrap samples and for each sample, $b=1,\ldots,B$, compute $\hat{\theta}^*=T(X_{1,b}^*,\ldots,X_{n,b}^*)$.

\subsection{Bootstrap percentile method}
We can use the empirical distribution of $\hat{\theta}^*$ to approximate the asymptotic distribution of $\hat{\theta}$.
For instance, the \KeyIdea{$100\times(1-\alpha)\%$ confidence interval for $\theta$ based on $\left(\hat{\theta}_{\frac{\alpha}{2}}^*,\hat{\theta}^*_{1-\frac{\alpha}{2}}\right)$},
where $\hat{\theta}^*_{\alpha}$ is the emprical percentile $\alpha\times 100$ of $\hat{\theta}^*_b$. This is called the \Term{bootstrap percentile method}.\\

In the \Term{i.i.d. bootstrap method} proposed by Efron (1979), the bootstrap sample $(X_1^*,\ldots,X_n^*)$ is obtained by drawing \Emph{samples with replacement} from the original sample $(X_1,\ldots,X_n)$,
where the probability of obtaining each $X_i$, $i=1,\ldots,n$, is $\frac{1}{n}$. \Emph{Denote $\bar{X}$ the average of the sample.}
Bickel and Freedman (1981) and Singh (1981) proved that if the elements of the \Emph{original sample were i.i.d.}, the bootstrap distribution $\sqrt{n}(\bar{X}^*-\bar{X})$ is close to the asymptotic distribution of $\sqrt{n}(\bar{X}-\mu)$ for large $n$.
This result justifies the validity of the bootstrap percentile method to construct a $100 \times (1-\alpha)\%$ confidence interval for the \Emph{mean}:
\begin{equation*}
    (\bar{X}^*_{\frac{\alpha}{2}},\bar{X}^*_{1-\frac{\alpha}{2}})
\end{equation*}
where $\bar{X}^*$ is the quantitle $100\times\alpha$ of $\bar{X}^*$.
\Info{$\underbrace{(\hat{\theta}-Z_{\frac{\alpha}{2}}SE(\hat{\theta}),\hat{\theta}+Z_{\frac{\alpha}{2}}SE(\hat{\theta}))}_{\text{asymptotically derived}}\approx \underbrace{(\hat{\theta}_{\frac{\alpha}{2}},\hat{\theta}_{1-\frac{\alpha}{2}})}_{\text{bootstrapping}}$}.\\

We can use it to test hypothesis \Emph{if the alternative is two-sided}: \KeyIdea{$H_0: \theta = \theta_0$ vs $H_1: \theta\neq\theta_0$} at $100\times\alpha\%$ level.
\Emph{Rejection rule}: We reject $H_0$ in favor of $H_1$ if $\theta_0\notin (\hat{\theta}^*_{\frac{\alpha}{2}}, \hat{\theta}^*_{1-\frac{\alpha}{2}})$.
There is no guarantee that the bootstrap percentile method performs better than the one obtained using asymptotic theory.

\subsection{Boostrap standard-errors}

The bootstrap estimator of $\theta$ is $\bar{\theta}^*_B = \frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\hat{\theta}^*_b$. The $s^2_{\hat{\theta}}$ can be approximated using
\begin{equation}
    s^2_{boot}= \frac{1}{B-1}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\left(\hat{\theta}^*_b - \bar{\theta}^*_B\right)^2
\end{equation}
Although more straight forward to compute than $s^2_{\hat{\theta}}$, there is not much work showing \Emph{consistentency} of this in general setting.
Even when consistent, there is no guarantee that this performs better than $s^2_{\hat{\theta}}$.
Hahn and Liao (2021) showed that $n\times s^2_{boot}$ is \Emph{never smaller} than $\lambda^2$ as $n\to\infty$.
Consequently, if one uses the statistic $\frac{(\hat{\theta}-\theta)}{s_{boot}}$ to make inference, it will \Emph{produce large confidence intervals and tests with size smaller than $\alpha$}$\to$ the tests will not reject $H_0$ very often.
\Info{Inference will be incorrect and should avoid using $s_{boot}$.}

\section{Boostrap with asymptotic refinement}

\Term{Asymptotic refinement} refers to the ability of the boostrap to provide approximations to the distributions of statistics that are more accurate than the approximations of conventional asymptotic distribution theory.
This implies that the actual size of the tests based on boostrap converges faster to $\alpha$ than the actual size of the tests based on the conventional asymptotic distribution theory.
\Info{$P(N(0,1)>Z_{\alpha})=\alpha\Rightarrow P(|\hat{t}|>Z_{\frac{\alpha}{2}})=\alpha + \frac{C_N}{\sqrt{N}}$ using asymptotic theory, only approximates to $N(0,1)$, converges asymptotically in distribution.}
We need to \Emph{assume} that the statistic of interest is a \KeyIdea{smooth function} of the data and that it is \KeyIdea{asymptotically pivotal}.
\begin{definition}
    An \textbf{asymptotically pivotal statistic} means that the limit distribution does not depend on unknown parameters.
\end{definition}
Most test statistics are asymptotically pivotal but most estimators are not.
\begin{Example}
    $H_0: \theta = \theta_0$, $\sqrt{n}(\hat{\theta} - \theta_0)\xrightarrow{d} N(0,\lambda^2)$. This is not asymptotically pivotal as the distribution depends on $\lambda$.\\
    $H_0: \theta = \theta_0$, $t = \frac{(\hat{\theta} - \theta_0)}{s_{\hat{\theta}}}\xrightarrow{d} N(0,1)$. This is asymptotically pivotal.
\end{Example}

We first generate $B$ different bootstrap samples and for each sample, $b=1,\ldots,B$, compute $\hat{t}_b = \frac{(\hat{\theta}^*_{b}-\hat{\theta})}{s^*_{\hat{\theta}_b}}$.
Note that we still need to obtain $s^*_{\hat{\theta}_b}$ which is the standard error computed with the bootstrap sample.

\subsection{One sided test for t-statistic}

Consider \Emph{$H_0: \theta = \theta_0$ vs $H_1: \theta > \theta_0$}.
We define the bootstrap critical value $cv_{boot}(1-\alpha)$ as the percentile \KeyIdea{$100\times(1-\alpha)$ of $(\hat{t}_1,\ldots,\hat{t}_B)$}.
\Emph{Rejection rule}: We reject $H_0$ in favor of $H_1$ if $\hat{t}_{obs}>cv_{boot}(1-\alpha)$, where $\hat{t}_{obs}$ is the observed value of the statistic in the sample.
The actual size of the test is $\alpha + O(n^{-1})$ which is \Emph{better than $\alpha+O(n^{-1/2})$}.

\subsection{Two sided test for t-statistic}

Consider \Emph{$H_0: \theta = \theta_0$ vs $H_1: \theta \neq \theta_0$}. We have two ways to define the bootstrap critical values.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \Term{non-symmetric test}: obtain the boostrap critical value \KeyIdea{$cv_{boot}(\frac{\alpha}{2})$ and $cv_{boot}(1-\frac{\alpha}{2})$} as the percentiles
    $100 \times \frac{\alpha}{2}$ and $100\times(1-\frac{\alpha}{2})$ of $(\hat{t}_1,\ldots,\hat{t}_B)$ respectivlly. 
    \Emph{Rejection rule}: We reject $H_0$ in favor of $H_1$ if $\hat{t}_{obs}\notin (cv_{boot}(\frac{\alpha}{2}),cv_{boot}(1-\frac{\alpha}{2}))$.
    The actual size of the test is $\alpha+O(n^{-1})$. 
    \Info{Bootstrap doesn't perform better than using asymptotic theory.}
    \item \Term{symmetric test}: obtain the boostrap critical value \KeyIdea{$cv_{boot}(1-\alpha)$} as the percentile $100 \times (1-\alpha)$ of $(|\hat{t}_1|,\ldots,|\hat{t}_B|)$.
    \Emph{Rejection rule}: We reject $H_0$ in favor of $H_1$ if $|\hat{t}_{obs}|>cv^a_{boot}(1-\alpha)$.
    The actual size of the test is $\alpha+O(n^{-3/2})$ which is \Emph{better than asymptotic theory}.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{p-values}

Consider \Emph{$H_0: \theta = \theta_0$ vs $H_1: \theta > \theta_0$}. The p-value is defined as $p = \frac{\sum_{b=1}^{B}1(\hat{t}_b>\hat{t}_{obs})}{B}$.
Consider \Emph{$H_0: \theta = \theta_0$ vs $H_1: \theta \neq \theta_0$}. The p-value is defined as $p = \frac{\sum_{b=1}^{B}1(|\hat{t}_b|>|\hat{t}_{obs}|)}{B}$.
In both cases, \Emph{rejection rule} is we reject $H_0$ in favor of $H_1$ if $p < \alpha$.

\subsection{confidence interval}

We can construct \Emph{$100\times(1-\alpha)\%$ confidence interval} using bootstrap t-statistic
\begin{itemize}
    \item $(\hat{\theta}-s_{\hat{\theta}}cv_{boot}(1-\frac{\alpha}{2}),\hat{\theta}-s_{\hat{\theta}}cv_{boot}(\frac{\alpha}{2}))$
    \item $(\hat{\theta}-s_{\hat{\theta}}cv_{boot}^a(1-\alpha),\hat{\theta}+s_{\hat{\theta}}cv_{boot}^a(1-\alpha))$ \Info{$\to$ better}
\end{itemize}

\section{Bootstrap in the linear regression model}

Consider the linear regression model $y_i = x_i'\beta+u_i$, $E[u_i|x_i]=0$.
Let $\hat{\beta}$ denote the \Emph{OLS} estiamte of $\beta$ and define the \Emph{residuals} as $\hat{u}_i = y_i - x'_i\hat{\beta}$.

\subsection{Residual bootstrap}

Denoting by $u^*_i$ the \Emph{bootstrap errors}, generate the dependent variable as $y^*_i = x'_i\hat{\beta}+u^*_i,\; i=1,\ldots,n$.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \Term{Parametric residual bootstrap}: if the distribution of the errors is known and independnet of the regressors,
    bootstrap errors can be generated from it. 
    \item \Term{Semiparametric residual bootstrap}: if the errors are iid and independent of the regressors,
    bootstrap errors can be obtained as draws with replacement from the vector of estimation residuals $\hat{u}_i$.
\end{itemize}
\Info{Independence between $u_i$ and $x_i$ is a must condition.
Heteroskedasticity arises when $Var(u_i|x_i)=f(x_i)$, then cannot apply either of the methods.}

\subsection{Heteroskedasticity}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \Term{Bootstrap of the pairs}: The \KeyIdea{bootstrap observations $(y^*_i,x'^*_i)$} can be obtained as draws with replacement from the sample $\{(y_1,x'_1),\ldots,(y_n,x'_n)\}$.
    We obtain $n$ such observations.
    \Info{Draw the y's and x's together then can use maximum likelihood.}
    \item \Term{Wild-bootstrap}: A variant of semiparametric residual bootstrap.
    To preserve the link between the erros and the regressors, the bootstrap error for observation $i$ can be obtained as \KeyIdea{$u^*_i = f(\hat{u}_i)\epsilon^b_i$}, where 
    \begin{itemize}
        \item $f(u_i)$ is an appropriate function of the residuals, e.g. the identity function of $f(\hat{u}_i) = \hat{u}_i$.
        \Info{Normally just use the residuals.}
        \item $\epsilon_i^b$ is a random variable independent of $u$ and $x$ such that $E[\epsilon_i^b] = 0$ and $\Var(\epsilon_i^b)=1$.
        We can use the \Term{Rademacher distribution} for $\epsilon_i^b$ where $F_1 : \epsilon_i^b = \begin{cases}
            -1  &\text{with possibility } \frac{1}{2}\\
            1  &\text{with possibility } \frac{1}{2}
        \end{cases}$
    \end{itemize}
    \Info{For this method we can only use OLS estimation.}
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Heteroskedasticity and serial correlation}

\Term{Moving blocks bootstrap}: In the case the data are serially correlated, we draw \KeyIdea{overlapping blocks} of $\ell$ consecutive observations from the sample $\{(y_1,x'_1),\ldots,(y_n,x'_n)\}$ with replacement until we have a sample of size $n$.
\Info{When the observations are correlated, it doesn't make sense to draw individually, you want to keep the correlation. The issue is more with choosing the size of the block.}

\section{Number of bootstraps}

For iid bootstrap and a sample of size $n$ there are $n^n$ possible bootstrap samples of size $n$.
For \Emph{standard error} computation, Efron and Tibsharan (1993) say \KeyIdea{$B=50$} is often enough, and $B=200$ is almost always enough.
For hypothesis testing and confidence intervals at standard levels of statistical significance involve the tails of the distribution, 
so more repetitions are needed. 
For \Emph{hypothesis testing} at level choose $B$ so that \KeyIdea{$(B+1)\alpha$ is an integer}. Davidson and MacKinnon (1999, 2000) recommend $B\geq 399$ if $\alpha=0.05$ and $B=1499$ if $\alpha=0.01$.
In the case of \Emph{inference} on means using \Emph{t-statistics} and iid sample, Babu and Singh (1983) suggest \KeyIdea{$B = n(\log n)^2$}.

\section{Final remarks}

Although the bootstrap is often accurate, it is not always valid.
There are several examples where bootstrap leads to \Emph{invalid inferences}.
\begin{Example}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Distributions with heavy tails
        \item Inference about a parameter that is on the boundary of the parameter set
        \Info{$\to$ inference on $\sigma^2 in [0,\infty)$}
        \item Inference about the maximum or minimum of random variables
    \end{itemize}
\end{Example}
If the quantities involved are \Emph{not asymptotically pivotal}, the bootstrap procedures are not better than usual methods based on asymptotic theory but may be easier to implement.
